# Context playbooks

Situation-specific moves. Each section: what's usually going on for the other person, what works, what backfires.

---

## Dating apps and early texting

**Their side:** They're juggling several chats, skimming fast, and wary of low-effort or creepy messages. They want to feel seen, and they want it to be easy.

**Works**
- Openers tied to something specific in their profile, with an easy way to respond
- Matching their energy and length
- Light humor and genuine curiosity; sharing a bit about yourself, not only asking questions
- Suggesting a specific plan once there's momentum
- Taking a slow reply at face value (busy) unless it's a pattern

**Backfires**
- "Hey" / "wyd" / copy-paste lines
- Interview mode (question after question)
- Sexual comments early, comments on their body
- Double-texting and "?" after no reply
- Over-investing too early (paragraphs, "I feel like we have a real connection" on day 2)
- Getting upset at an unmatch or a fade

**Common moves**
- *Conversation dying:* switch topics to something from their profile, or suggest meeting up. Don't ask "why are you so dry?"
- *Asking out:* "I'm enjoying this, want to continue it over [specific thing] [day]?"
- *They say no to a date or "not feeling it":* "All good, thanks for being upfront. Good luck out there." Then stop.
- *Ghosted:* one light, no-pressure message after a few days at most, or nothing. Never guilt.
- *They're slow but engaged:* match their pace.

## Relationships and breakups

**Their side (when they pull back or end it):** Usually they've been thinking about it for a while. They feel guilt and dread your reaction. They need you to not make it harder.

**"I can't do this relationship" / "I think we should stop seeing each other"**
- The decision is theirs. Arguing, bargaining, or listing reasons they're wrong almost never works, and it makes them more sure.
- Best moves: acknowledge, thank them for honesty, say one true feeling briefly if the user wants to, and leave the door open (or closed) with dignity.
- It's fine to ask *once*, gently, if the user wants clarity ("Can I ask what changed? Totally okay if you'd rather not get into it."), and only if it was a real relationship, not a few dates.
- Suggest not replying instantly if the user is hurt. Draft now, send after a breath.
- Offer a version that asks for space ("I need some space before we talk again") if that's what the user needs.

**Fights and tension**
- De-escalate first, solve second. Watch for the Four Horsemen (`frameworks.md`).
- "I" statements and one specific issue, not a list of grievances.
- If it's getting long or heated over text: "I don't want to fight over text. Can we call later?"

**Ending it yourself**
- Be clear and kind, and don't leave fake hope. Short is kinder than long.
- For anything beyond a few dates, suggest a call or in person when safe.
- Don't blame them or give a list of their flaws.

**Exes reaching out**
- Figure out the user's goal first (closure, friends, back together, nothing). Then match it.

## Friends and family

**Their side:** History and expectations. Guilt and old patterns show up fast.

**Works**
- Warmth first, then the boundary or the no
- Saying no without a long excuse: "I can't make it this time, but I'd love to see you next week"
- Naming the pattern gently when something keeps happening
- For family across cultures: respect + clarity. Help the user say what they need without attacking the relationship.

**Backfires**
- Silent treatment, passive-aggressive group-chat comments
- Over-explaining a no (it invites negotiation)
- Dragging in others ("everyone thinks…")

## LinkedIn and networking

**Their side:** Busy, gets lots of asks, wants to help people who seem genuine and easy to help, and doesn't want to stake their name on a stranger.

**Cold outreach**
- Specific reason for them, small ask, easy yes. Shared background (same school, program, team) helps a lot.
- Never ask for a referral in the first message.

**After a coffee chat**
- Thank-you within 24 hours that names one specific thing you learned or will act on.
- If a referral was discussed: a separate, direct, easy ask with the job link and a 2–3 line pitch they can paste.

**"The chat was good but I didn't get a referral" / they went quiet after the chat**
- Their side: they may not know you well enough to vouch, may not have a fitting role, or simply got busy. A referral puts their name on the line.
- Don't guilt or re-pitch hard. Options:
  1. *Direct but easy ask* (if the chat genuinely went well and there's a specific role): "Would you be comfortable referring me for [role/link]? Totally understand if it's not the right fit."
  2. *Ask for direction instead*: "Is there anyone on the [team] you'd suggest I talk to?"
  3. *Nurture*: send a useful update in a few weeks (you applied, finished a relevant project, used their advice) and keep the relationship.
- One follow-up after about a week of silence, then move on and keep networking elsewhere.

**Comments on posts**
- Add value: a real takeaway, a thoughtful question, or a short related experience. Avoid "Congrats!!" alone or self-promotion.

## Job search

- **Recruiter outreach:** short, interested, and with a clear next step (availability, resume).
- **Rejection:** a gracious reply keeps the door open: thank them, express continued interest, ask to be considered for future roles. Optionally ask for one piece of feedback (don't expect it).
- **Ghosted after an interview:** one follow-up after the date they gave you passes (or about a week), then one more after another week max.
- **Offer negotiation:** enthusiasm first, then a specific ask with a reason (market data, competing offer), framed as solving it together. Never lie about a competing offer.
- **Declining an offer:** prompt, grateful, brief; no need to over-explain.

## School (professors, TAs, advisors)

**Their side:** Fairness to every student, policies, and an overloaded inbox. They respond best to students who are respectful, take responsibility, and make it easy to say yes.

**Asking for an extension**
- Check the syllabus first. Ask before the deadline, ideally several days before.
- Subject line with course and assignment. Brief, honest reason (no oversharing). Propose a specific new date. Note what's already done.

**"No, I can't give you an extension"**
- Their side: likely a fairness or policy issue, not personal. Pushing again usually hurts the relationship.
- Best reply: accept graciously, thank them, and if it helps, ask one useful thing: partial-credit or late-work policy, office hours to get unstuck, or how to prioritize what's left.
- Only push back if there's a real documented reason (illness, emergency, disability accommodation). Then involve the right office (dean of students, disability services) rather than arguing.

**Grades**
- Ask to *understand*, not to dispute: "I'd like to understand where I lost points so I can improve."
- Point to specific rubric items if you believe there's an error.

**Recommendation letters**
- Ask at least 3–4 weeks ahead, give an easy out, then send a packet: resume, deadline, what it's for, and reminders of your work in their class.

**Group projects**
- Name the specific problem and a specific proposal, not blame. Loop in the professor only after trying directly, and with facts.

## Work (managers, coworkers)

**Their side (manager):** They're accountable upward. They care about predictability, ownership and solutions more than excuses.

**Manager: "You're late on this"**
- Own it in one line (no excuse-dump), give a realistic new ETA, say what you're doing to hit it, and flag any blocker they can help with.
- Example structure: "You're right, that's on me. I'll have it to you by [time]. [One line on what's left / what you need]. I'll [prevent it next time]."
- If you're late because of competing priorities they set, say it as a question: "Should I prioritize X or Y this week?"

**Asking for things** (raise, time off, help, a different project): BLUF, the reason in their terms, a specific proposal.

**Pushback / disagreeing:** acknowledge their goal, give your concern with facts, propose an option, defer if they decide ("your call").

**Mistakes:** tell them early, with what happened, the impact, the fix, and prevention.

**Coworkers:** go direct and private first, assume good intent, and be specific.

## Clients and customers

**Their side:** They're paying, they have their own boss or customers, and they hate surprises.

- **Saying no / out of scope:** acknowledge the request, explain the scope plainly, and offer a path (a quote, a phase two, a smaller version).
- **Late payment:** friendly but factual: invoice number, amount, due date, how to pay. Escalate tone slowly across reminders.
- **Bad news (delay, mistake):** tell them early, own it, give the new plan, and make it right.
- **Angry client:** acknowledge the frustration, don't argue the details in the first reply, propose a call, and follow with a written summary.

## Apologies (any context)

Use the apology structure in `frameworks.md`. Match the size of the apology to the size of the mistake. A big apology for a small thing makes it weird; a tiny one for a big thing makes it worse.
