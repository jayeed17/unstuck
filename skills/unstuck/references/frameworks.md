# Frameworks

Use these as thinking tools. Never name the framework in the user's message. Nobody wants a text that reads like a worksheet.

## DBT interpersonal priorities

Before any hard message, decide what matters most:

| Priority | Question | Skill |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | What do I want to happen? | DEAR MAN |
| Relationship | How do I want them to feel about me after? | GIVE |
| Self-respect | How do I want to feel about myself after? | FAST |

When they conflict, say so. Example: pushing a professor a third time on an extension might win the objective but cost the relationship and some self-respect.

### DEAR MAN (asking for something, or saying no)
- **Describe** the facts, no judgment
- **Express** how you feel or why it matters (briefly; in professional settings, express impact instead of feelings)
- **Assert** the ask or the no, clearly
- **Reinforce**: why it's good for them too, or thanks
- **Mindful**: stay on the point; repeat calmly if needed
- **Appear confident**: no groveling, no over-explaining
- **Negotiate**: offer an alternative ("If not Friday, would Monday work?")

### GIVE (keeping the relationship)
- **Gentle**: no attacks, threats or judgment
- **Interested**: ask about their side
- **Validate**: acknowledge their feelings or constraints out loud
- **Easy manner**: a little lightness where it fits

### FAST (keeping self-respect)
- **Fair** to yourself and them
- **(no excessive) Apologies**: apologize once, for real things
- **Stick to values**: don't say things you don't mean to get a yes
- **Truthful**: no made-up excuses

## Nonviolent Communication (NVC)

For conflict and emotional messages: **Observation** (facts, no judgment) → **Feeling** → **Need** → **Request**.
"When plans changed last minute twice this week (observation), I felt kind of brushed off (feeling). I want to know I'm a priority (need). Can we lock in Saturday? (request)"
Drop the formula feel; it should sound natural.

## Acknowledge → Bridge → Ask

The everyday version for hard replies:
1. **Acknowledge** their message or constraint genuinely
2. **Bridge** to shared ground or the user's point
3. **Ask** or close clearly

## BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)

For work, school and client email: the ask or the key point goes in the first 1–2 sentences, then context. Subject lines say what the email is.

## SBI (giving feedback)

**Situation** → **Behavior** (observable) → **Impact**. "In Tuesday's client call (S), the numbers in slide 4 were from last quarter (B), so they asked us to redo the deck (I)."

## Gottman: the Four Horsemen and their antidotes

For relationships and close conflict, watch for these in both the user's draft and the other person's:

| Horseman | Looks like | Antidote |
|---|---|---|
| Criticism | "You always / you never" | Gentle start-up: "I feel… about… I need…" |
| Contempt | Sarcasm, eye-roll energy, mocking | Appreciation and respect |
| Defensiveness | "Well you…", excuses | Take responsibility for your part |
| Stonewalling | Going silent, "whatever" | Ask for a pause: "I need a bit to think — can we talk at 8?" |

**Repair attempts:** small moves that de-escalate ("ok that came out wrong", "can we restart?", humor if it fits). Help the user make one and notice the other person's.

## Perspective-taking prompts

Run through these silently in Step 3:
- What's their day or week probably like?
- What are they afraid will happen if they say yes? If they say no?
- What would they be embarrassed or annoyed to receive?
- What do they owe the user, really, and what don't they?
- If the roles were flipped, what would the user want to hear?

## Apology structure

1. Name what you did, specifically
2. Own the impact, without "if" or "but"
3. Say what you'll do differently or how you'll fix it
4. Don't demand forgiveness or a fast reply

Skip the self-flagellation. It makes the other person comfort you.
